Introduction
Social media is a relatively inexpensive and readily available electronic resource that
allows anyone to publish and access content and cooperate on a collective effort,
becoming an essential part of modern culture, affecting all races, sexes, socioeconomic
backgrounds, and people all over the world. It is becoming apparent that social
networking websites have become an integral part of people’s lives. As a result, there is
a need for people to adjust their lifestyles as emerging technologies evolve. These
platforms have been a part of people’s daily lives, with many applications ranging from
education to corporate contact, marketing, healthcare, and even dating.
Social media is a form of web-based data communication where users may hold
discussions, exchange information, and generate web material. Individually, allowing
people to connect with our friends and families, learn new knowledge, cultivate our
passions, and be entertained (Akram & Kumar, 2017). On a professional level, it can
extend or deepen expertise in a specific field and develop a professional network by
engaging with other professionals within the industry (Ji et al., 2010). Social media is a
new concept with tremendous growth potential. Similarly, people no longer need to
depend on the newspapers or television to get their regular news consumption; it can all
be accessed on a social networking site (Bolier et al., 2013). As a result, studying all the
possible dangers and advantages is critical because it will offer a well-rounded solution
to improving our present position in an environment where social networking can cause
harms such as addiction or cyberbullying.
The objective of this study is to focus on literature from both small and large studies
examining the harms and advantages of the online environment, to identify the line at
which point casual social media use may become dangerous and to highlight possible
avoidance techniques to enable the virtual world to become less harmful.

Social media use
In the 2006 Parents and Teens survey, a random sample of 935 participants
demonstrated that 55% of youths used SNSs (social networking sites) in that year.
Furthermore, they also investigated that 91% used social media to stay in touch with
friends and 49% to make new friends (more common in boys than girls) (Lenhart &
Madden, 2007). The most common activities on SNSs were reading, responding to
comments (60%) and posting comments, links, responding to messages (14%), and
browsing profiles (13%) (Pempek et al., 2009).
In another study discussing how people interact with SNSs using psychophysiological
measures (skin conductance and facial electromyography), media scanning (extracting
material from profiles) was found to be more rewarding than social surfing (passive
reading of news feeds) (Bolls et al., 2009). Consequently, the results indicate that goal-
directed social seeking can activate the appetitive system rather than the aversive system
due to a genetic defect in the addict’s neurochemical reward system (Uylings et al., 2000).
According to the gratification hypothesis, media is seen as a goal-directed means of
gratification and need fulfilment and is comparable to addiction. People with higher social
identity (i.e., collective solidarity/conformity), altruism, and telepresence are more likely
to use SNSs because they perceive support for social network participation (Kwon & Wen,
2010). A survey of 170 university students found that social influences were more
important motivators for SNS use than human factors (Kim et al., 2010).
Another study found that collective self-esteem and group identification positively
correlated with peer group communication via SNSs, suggesting that people being part
of the same virtual realm, the support of group values, the preservation of interpersonal
interconnectivity, and social change were some of the reasons for SNS usage (Cheung
et al., 2011).
The participating university student sample endorsed the following motivations:
● To keep in contact with friends they do not often see. (81%)
● To conform to other people/friends who have them be part of a group. (61%)
● To keep in contact with relatives and family. (48%)
● To make plans with friends they often see. (35%)
Overall, it appears that the advantages of bridging social capital created by engagement
in SNSs were especially beneficial for people with low self-esteem (Kujath, 2011).
Furthermore, due to many possible low social links between participants, which are
allowed by digital media’s structural features, SNSs are thought to increase a potential4
networks’ size (Donath & Boyd, 2004). Specifically, maintaining social capital via
participation in SNSs is more beneficial to students regarding potential employment
opportunities and sustaining ties with old friends. Thus, one explanation why people with
low self-esteem are attracted to using SNSs in a potentially excessive way is the ease of
creating and retaining bridging social capital and, in combination with lower self-esteem,
has been demonstrated to be related to Internet addiction (Armstrong et al., 2000).
A study on SNS usage between different cultures (the USA vs Korea vs China) has
demonstrated that using different Facebook functions was associated with creating and
maintaining either bridging or bonding social capital. For example, people in the US used
SNSs for communication, whereas Koreans and Chinese used SNSs for expert search
(i.e., searching for professionals online) and ‘Connection’ (i.e., maintaining offline
relationships) (Ji et al., 2010).
A student survey study of 387 participants found the predictive factors were: playfulness
(i.e., enjoyment and pleasure), the critical mass of the users who endorsed the
technology, trust in the site, perceived ease of use, and perceived usefulness
(Sledgianowski & Kulviwat, 2009).
The studies contained small convenience samples, teenagers, or university students as
participants, therefore severely limiting the generalisability of findings.
Using the gratification theory demonstrates how popular social media is in today’s world
and how social media as a whole is tailored to our social needs. It is especially important
now to stay in touch online for the evolution of friendships, not just one-on-one friendships,
but also as a group and for personal and business purposes worldwide. As a result, 81%
of social media users use it to communicate with current friends they do not see very
much. SNSs also provide introverted individuals with the opportunity for better self-
expression.5
Social media and neuronal systems
According to the study by Meshi et al. (2015), social media serves as an online platform
for meeting basic social needs via five critical strategies. These five behaviours: Users
broadcasting information, receiving feedback, observing, providing feedback and
engaging in social comparison, depend on three domains. The three domains are
cognitive processing (mentalising), self-referential cognition, and social reward
processing. According to the mentalising network, social media allows one to consider
other people’s emotional states/motivations. The self-referential cognition network
provides and embraces input expressing self-evaluation and social distinction. Finally, the
incentive network implies that social media offers a consistent supply of social incentives
and reinforcement in positive feedback or suggestions, resulting in credibility
enhancement. Because of the minimalistic cue of social performance, both stimuli
stimulate the reward system (Meshi et al., 2015).
Social rewards activate a network of brain regions, including the ventromedial prefrontal
cortex (VMPFC), the ventral striatum, and the ventral tegmental lobe (Fareri & Delgado,
2014). This knowledge has been extended to practical values by connecting social media
stimuli and brain activation regions to observe the social values people have outside of
social media. Exchanging information with others stimulates the VMPFC and ventral
striatum. Receiving positive social reinforcement (e.g., signals that people understand
you, agree with you or like you) and providing others with the same social incentives (e.g.,
offering a ‘like’) is a form of prosocial behaviour triggering the reward mechanism (such
as donating to charity). Reading other people’s posts offers the same reward activity as
acquiring information and is followed by activity in the ventral striatum. According to
analysis, the ventral striatum also underpins social comparison, which suggests that
behaviour in this area shows the comparison between one’s reward and that of another,
which could suggest an explanation to the form of jealousy people experience whilst on
social media (Fareri & Delgado, 2014).
Social media’s main advantages are that it allows communication not constrained by
spatial and temporal distance. However, people are likely to be deprived of social cues
such as close interaction, e.g., seeing micro-expressions and feeling physical contact. It
was observed that individuals with more extensive online social networks have a larger
amygdala than individuals with smaller social networks, which suggests a physiological
reason for the attraction of humans to the use of social media (Meshi et al., 2015).
A study examining the relationship between online social media use and the neural
processing of offline credibility benefits showed that the nucleus accumbent reaction to
reputation enhancement predicted participants’ social media use intensity. These findings
support these processes’ role in the sustainability of social networks and add to our6
knowledge of the factors that influence social behaviour. More specifically, they
demonstrate the feasibility of studying social neurocognitive processes using metrics
created by social media use (Meshi et al., 2013).
Collectively, these findings exhibit how social media is tailored and adapted to the human
evolutionary urge to socialise, learn, and communicate with groups of people. Thus,
neuropsychology data explains how people are programmed to communicate and how
social media networks are designed to satisfy and even exceed those needs, providing
the backbone to both potential harms research and social networks’ overall usefulness
and functionality.7
Social media addiction
In recent years, with the development of information technology, especially the rapid
spread of internet-based social media (e.g., Facebook, WeChat, or Instagram), the ways
of interpersonal communication have dramatically changed (Anderson & Smith, 2018).
The ubiquitous social media platforms and easy access to the internet give rise to the
potential for social media addiction, i.e., the irrational and excessive use of social media
to the extent that it interferes with other daily aspects of life (Griffiths, 2009). There is
considerable evidence demonstrating a connection between social media addiction and
a range of emotional, interpersonal, health, and performance problems (Griffiths, 2000).
Social media addiction is a type of internet addiction in which people feel compelled to
use social media in excessive amounts (Griffiths, 2000). Individuals addicted to social
media are often unnecessarily worried and are motivated by an uncontrollable compulsion
to log in to utilise it (Andreassen & Pallesen, 2014). According to studies, mood, cognition,
physical and emotional reactions, and interpersonal and psychological problems are all
social media addiction symptoms (Błachnio et al., 2017). According to reports,
approximately 12% of social networking sites users suffer from social media addiction
(Wu et al., 2013).
Recent literature on social media usage and mental wellbeing has shown that long-term
use of social media platforms like Facebook is positively correlated with mental health
problems like stress, anxiety, and depression while negatively associated with long-term
wellbeing (Eraslan-Capan, 2015). Time spent on social media, for example, was linked
to depressive symptoms in high school students in Central Serbia (Pantic et al., 2012)
and young adults in the United States (Lin et al., 2016).
The argument about whether so-called internet addiction should be accepted as a
disease in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition is
ongoing. The correlation between “addiction” and various compulsive or impulsive
activities is often a source of misunderstanding. Some psychologists claim that internet
addiction has the same traits as many other substance use disorders, such as heavy use,
withdrawal effects, tolerance, and harmful consequences; however, there is no
physiological research to support these arguments. Thus, it is uncertain if internet
addiction is a symptom of an underlying condition or a different illness category. In
addition, the recurrent emergence of internet addiction in the sense of a wide range of
comorbidities poses difficult causality questions (Pies, 2009).
A more generic model of what qualifies as “disease” is required and as a particular
disease to make nosological conclusions on internet addiction. It remains premature to
recognise internet addiction as a distinct disease category, based on a model addressing
intrinsic distress and incapacity and evidence on course, prognosis, temporal8
consistency, and recovery response. However, recent evidence shows that certain people
addicted to the internet are at severe risk and need immediate support. To overcome
these disputes, closely conducted studies are needed (Pies, 2009).9
Social media and cell phone addiction
Addiction can be anything if there is a possibility of negative consequences and physical
and psychological reinforcements in a specific context – cell phone and social media
addiction would be classed as a behavioural addiction. In the case of behaviour, it is
difficult to determine whether problems result from problematic behaviour, personality
traits, or psychiatric comorbidities (De-Sola Gutiérrez et al., 2016). Young, (1999) studied
five different forms of addictive behaviour on the internet: firstly, the computer itself, the
act of searching for information, interaction compulsions, such as contact with the web
through online games, shopping, etc., cyber sexuality, and cyber contacts (Young, 1999).
In general, it is noted that addiction entails abuse without control, alterations in mood,
tolerance, abstinence, personal harm or conflicts in the environment, and a tendency to
relapse (Griffiths, 1996). The Cell Age Telephone initiation is getting younger as about
30% of 10-year-old Spanish children have a mobile phone, 70% of 12-year-olds and 83%
have a cell phone by the age of 14, causing behavioural problems and disorders in teens.
As in general, the youngest group, mainly adolescents, are the most highly affected by
and at risk for substance and behavioural addictions (Protégeles, 2014). Furthermore,
this has become more evident within the communications media, inspiring new
pathologies, such as “Nomophobia” (No-Mobile-Phobia), “FOMO” (Fear Of Missing Out),
the fear of being deprived of a cell phone, disconnected or off the internet. It is further
extending to false sensations such as “Textaphrenia” and “Ringxiety” – which is thinking
that one has received a text message or a phone call when they have not, causing them
to be continually checking the device (Taneja, 2014). Moreover, “Textiety” – the anxiety
of receiving and responding immediately to text messages. Compared to and
corroborated by the diagnostic criteria of the DSM, problematic manifestations have
suggested that when in unsafe circumstances or forbidden contexts, problematic and
deliberate use is a sign of an arising addiction (Billieux, 2008).
In behavioural terms, these problematic behaviours have been highlighted, compared to
and commonly validated by the diagnostic criteria of the DSM: Social and familial conflicts
and confrontations and the loss of interest in other activities (Griffiths, 2000), harm
repeated physical, mental, social, work, or familial interruptions, preferring the cell phone
to personal contact (Chesley, 2005), other addiction-like behaviours such as excessive
use, urgency, tolerance, dependence, difficulty controlling, craving, increasing use to
achieve satisfaction or relaxation, or counteracting a dysphoric mood (Kwon et al., 2013),
anxiety and loneliness when unable to send a message or receive immediate response
stress (Ha et al., 2008) and mood changes due to the need to respond immediately to
messages (Sansone & Sansone, 2013).10
Sociodemographic differences in cell phone dependency
Age
The data suggest that the average time spent on mobile phones declines with age, with
people younger than 20 years of age showing the highest hours, mainly teens under 14
(Lopez-Fernandez et al., 2014). This phenomenon has been suggested to be due to
decreased self-control observed at that age, due to underdeveloped structures in the
brain which affect self-control (Bianchi & Phillips, 2005). The most frequent use of the
mobile phone is in the applications which allow text messaging (Lu et al., 2011).
Cell phones have been observed to be so vital in an adolescent’s life that between the
ages of 13-14, one out of three never turns off their phone (Protégeles, 2014).
Unfortunately, this has been found to link with other problematic addictions in the future,
especially when the first phone was obtained before the age of 13 years (Sahın et al.,
2013).
Gender
Most research suggests that women have higher levels of dependency and problematic
usage than men (Jenaro et al., 2007). Where usually, female cell phone usage is
connected to sociability (Bianchi & Phillips, 2005).
There were differences observed between female and male usage time rather than
utilisation. Females have been observed to spend more time on applications that increase
intimacy with other people, orientated towards intense and close relationships. In
comparison, males tend to spend more time on applications for practical and instrumental
reasons (Roberts et al., 2014).
The formation and management of connections, interpersonal relationships, indirect
communication, and texting are the most frequently used applications for both sexes.
However, there was a difference observed in cell phone and internet usage. Whilst
females use cell phones as a means of social contact; they have less frequent internet
usage. Hence, problematic internet behaviour is more frequently observed in males (Ruiz-
Olivares et al., 2010).
Education, Cultural and Economic
Effects of culture and economic status on the dependence on social media were observed
in students. It was found that students from higher cultural and economic levels showed
higher levels of dependence. This was suggested to be due to more frequent isolation
due to studying, where a cell phone is a tool for contact (Amidi Mazaheri & Karbasi, 2014).
A significant association was observed between parents’ abuse and dependence on cell11
phones and children’s addiction to the internet and other technologies. The correlation
has been suggested to be due to emotional abandonment (Zhou et al., 2014).
Personality and Psychological Variables
The studies regarding problematic cell phone and internet usage focus on the five-factor
model (FFM) of personality, self-esteem, self-concept, self-identity and impulsivity.
Personality characteristic studies have observed that extroverts use social media
networks to form new and improve old relationships. In contrast, introverts use social
networks to compensate for human interaction. Both introverts and extroverts have the
potential to become dependent. However, it has been found that extroverts with low
scores in conscientiousness and introverts with high scores in neuroticism and narcissism
have the highest potential to become addicted (Kuss & Griffiths, 2011). Neuroticism was
a significant factor as it results in high levels of anxiety and insecurity (Correa et al., 2010).
Problems derived from cell phone abuse
The research focuses on sleep disruption and its coexistence with the use of substances
such as alcohol and tobacco and with symptomatology and psychiatric comorbidities,
particularly anxiety, stress, and depression, concerning the psychological problems
resulting from cell phone misuse.
It was found that problematic student cell phone usage, especially in females, correlated
with high levels of both anxiety and insomnia (Jenaro et al., 2007). A link was observed
between the number of phone calls and messages with sleep difficulties and a tendency
to use cell phones at night (Thomée et al., 2007). Cell phone abuse has been linked with
personal stress as it maintains a constant state of alertness hence interferes with sleep
(Sansone & Sansone, 2013). Similarly, with social networks, excessive Facebook usage
has been associated with interruption of sleep during the week and decreasing the
number of hours slept (Andreassen et al., 2012).
Overall, it is evident how social media can easily and quickly grasp people across all
ages, genders, socioeconomic groups and cultures. Furthermore, it also demonstrates
how they can become dependent and what effects it can cause (as observed in figure 2).12

Social media use in healthcare
The first research on social media impact was focused on experiences with patients and
healthcare providers and the effect of social media on their wellbeing. Seven types of
effects on patients were identified: improved self-management and control, enhanced
psychological and subjective wellbeing, diminished subjective wellbeing, addiction to
social media, loss of privacy, and being targeted for promotion. The findings revealed that
the primary motive for using social media was for social support. Furthermore, it led to
equal communication between the patient and healthcare professional, increased
doctors’ switching, harmonious relationships, and suboptimal interaction between the
patient and healthcare professional (Smailhodzic et al., 2016).
The social media support was defined as “the process of interaction in relationships which
is intended to improve coping, esteem, belonging, and competence through actual or
perceived exchanges of psychosocial resources” (Cohen et al., 2000) as it was observed
that within the social media support it covered four different areas: emotional support,
esteem support, information support, and network support. Patients who used social
media during their illness had more opportunities to connect with others in a similar
situation, reducing loneliness (Colineau & Paris, 2010). Moreover, social media provided
an opportunity for patients to discuss the sensitive characteristics of their condition as
using online communities reduces the inhibitions felt during sharing experiences face-to-
face (Broom, 2005). Thus, patients who used social media for network support exhibited
enhanced psychological wellbeing. Despite this, it was revealed that social media could
also harm a patient’s wellbeing. It was observed that social media could trigger
unnecessary jealousy and anxiety when patients took a ‘lurker’ role on social media, only
using it to read stories and compare, therefore illustrating how social media’s effects are
highly dependent on the participant rather than the platform. It can be a haven for the
patient to unload their problems and develop coping strategies, or it can be a source of
anxiety and potential triggers (Smailhodzic et al., 2016).
The study provides some critical points to determine how vital social media can be and
how it can produce a positively transformative medium when used correctly. However, it
also demonstrates how damaging it can also be for individuals when misused. Therefore,
supplying a platform where various patients can discuss and advise each other whilst
minimising potential damage by preventing lurking will be an important development
within the social media realm.14
Social media use with students
The research looked at how social media and social media multitasking (SMM) affect the
academic success of university students. The research comprised a group of 348
undergraduate students at Hong Kong’s comprehensive university. The research
revealed that social media use and SMM are two main variables that detrimentally affect
university students’ academic success, specifically student learning (Lau, 2017). Cheever
et al. (2015) proposed strategies such as technology breaks, self-monitoring, teaching
metacognitive skills, and encouraging technology awareness to help students navigate
their use of technology, allowing social media to reach its full beneficiary potential.
Social media is said to have a detrimental academic performance effect (Jacobsen &
Forste, 2011) but can also improve student learning and ‘cognitive capacity’ within the
social context (Bell, 2011). For example, in students engaging in forums, it is assumed
that since students are engaged and interested in such forums, these channels would
encourage students to think more critically and objectively (Jones 2011).
Furthermore, social networking facilitates collaborative learning (such as peer-to-peer
and lecturer-to-many students). Since social media forums produce a long-lasting public
message, discussions should be more constructive and thoughtful (Hsiao et al., 2013).
As evidence suggests that more critical thinking occurs, online communities deliver more
significant interaction, informative points, and personal reflection on both theory and
practice (Meyer 2004). Despite this, the findings indicate that less social learning exists
through online conversations compared to face-to-face discussions.
In the world of adolescents, social media and learning do not seem to go hand in hand,
likely due to the apparent element of imminent distraction. However, an opportunity might
exist for introverted students who lack the courage to speak up in class or for more
extroverted personalities who could not do enough critical thinking before speaking up if
the risk of distraction was eliminated and there is enough incentive to engage. A site with
the ability to cater to both could revolutionise online education.15
Social media effects on welfare
Bolier et al. (2013) produced research that looked at the implications of turning off
Facebook for four weeks. A sample of 2,743 people and their willingness to agree (WTA)
to get their Facebook accounts deactivated for four weeks was observed.
The sample was split into two groups: a recovery group paid $102 and a monitoring group
not paid to deactivate. It was observed that those who reduced online contact, thereby
increasing offline activities such as watching TV alone and socialising with family and
friends, demonstrated behaviours that: reduced both objective news and political
polarisation, improved subjective wellbeing and resulted in a significant and lasting
decrease in Facebook post-experiment usage. In addition, deactivation resulted in small
but important wellbeing improvements such as self-reported happiness, personal
satisfaction, and less depression and anxiety. The overall index of subjective wellbeing
improved by 0.09 standard deviations – point of comparison used about 25–40% of the
effect of psychological interventions including self-help therapy, group training, and
individual therapy, as reported in a meta-analysis by (Bolier et al., 2013).
Facebook’s deactivation increased subjective wellbeing, and 80% of the Recovery
Community said the deactivation phase was successful for them. However, the
participants could not give up Facebook unless they received reasonably significant sums
of money: even after being deactivated for four weeks, which should have facilitated at
least some learning or “detox” from addiction (Bolier et al., 2013). According to the results
on the usage of Facebook, Facebook is a significant source of news and knowledge. In
interviews, it was clear that Facebook would affect people’s lives in various ways, either
as a source of entertainment, a way to coordinate a charity or activist group, or a vital
social lifeline for those who are otherwise alone. Despite the drawbacks of social media,
it seems to satisfy essential and universal needs (Bolier et al., 2013).
It offers some critical insights into the advantages of avoiding social media and
concentrating on other activities such as spending time with friends and relatives and
improving general wellbeing. Despite this, the four-week cleanse demonstrates its impact
on people’s lives, as shown by the vast number of people returning to social media.16
Social media use in dating
The dating industry has over 2 billion users, with the first domain coming into existence
in 1995 and being studied systematically from 2005 (Wiederhold, 2015). There have been
multiple advantages observed in using online platforms to find a prospective partner, such
as broader prospects to meet potential partners, less intimidation to initiate contact, and
the possibility that the questions/criteria can open someone’s mind to more
possibilities/criteria out of their comfort zones. However, there are also disadvantages
such as objectifying nature, the possibility that the domain users may form bad decisions
based on many choices, the potential to delay face-to-face contact for too long, and
misinterpretation of messages (Finkel et al., 2012).
Ultimately, the dating domains should only be used as a tool to find a potential partner,
as genuine relationships can only begin after face-to-face contact. Research into online
dating has demonstrated that eventually, it will reside at the intersection of experiential
attributes, chemistry, and gut-level evaluations. Specific emotional reactions are based
on sensory experiences such as olfaction, which cannot be obtained any other way. In-
person meeting acts as a reality check before intimacy can progress further, as people
are less likely to misrepresent observable attributes in real-life settings (Finkel et al.,
2012).
The goal of using these domains can vary. Daters looking for a temporary relationship
often increase their expectations, whereas those looking to move the relationship
permanently offline tend to decrease their expectations. In addition, daters who initiate
communication are more likely to find suitable partners than those waiting to be asked
(Kreager et al., 2014). Even though there are not many studies following daters long-term,
the results from one large US national study (N = 19,131) demonstrated that one-third of
marriages now begin online. It was also observed that those marriages which began
online were less likely to result in divorce and were associated with slightly higher marital
satisfaction than those that started in traditional offline venues. This data demonstrates
that the internet alters the dynamics and outcomes of marriage (Cacioppo et al., 2013).
There are now multiple domains offering different types of relationship support. Apps such
as Nayana are used to predict seven years of romantic outlook. The app predicts that
someone who has remained in the same location for five years or is an introvert is less
likely to meet people than someone who has just moved or is an extrovert. Another app,
The Grade, gives grades to users based on their dating behaviour, giving them letter
grades and suggestions for improvements. Those who ignore their advice get kicked out.
There is a gap in research on online dating behaviour in different cultures, such as in
India. Lastly, technological advances in online dating cannot replace the actual work
required for an intimate relationship to advance (Wiederhold, 2015).17
Study on Tinder
Tinder is currently the most popular dating app with certain advantages over traditional
dating methods, such as enhanced portability of access (being an app) and the
geolocalisation capacity (Schrock, 2015). Due to its high popularity, it enables increased
access to potential matches. It is best in class for its simple and user-friendly interface
design, with the most popular feature being the ‘swipe’ matching characteristic. It entices
users by providing the stimulation of localised likes, making it easy for a potential meeting.
However, compared to apps outside of the dating sphere, these features have an
increased potential for a behavioural addiction due to this added element of romance
associated with the use of an artificial domain (Orosz et al., 2016). The Uses and
Gratifications Theory has shown the reason for the popularity of Tinder is because it fulfils
physical (e.g. sexual desire), social (e.g. finding a partner or a friend) and psychosocial
needs (e.g., validating one’s physical attractiveness). Studies also suggest that tinder
consumption may be used as a coping mechanism to regulate depressive effects and
improve self-esteem (Orosz et al., 2018) with variable other motives depending on gender
(Ranzini & Lutz, 2016).
The study consisted of 1,697 subjects, of whom 46.59% were females. All subjects
completed all questionnaires and reported a heterosexual orientation and were thus
retained in the final analysis. Subjects’ age ranged from 18 to 74 years old (M = 30.02,
SD = 9.19). In the study, four clusters were formed. Cluster 1, “regulated,” was the largest
group and showed low impulsivity and high self-esteem with a secure attachment, low
depressive mood and problematic use. Cluster 2, “regulated with low desire,” was the
smallest group that exhibited low/medium impulsivity, low self-esteem, anxious
attachment, low motives, more depressive mood but low problematic use. Cluster 3 was
“unregulated and highly motivated” and exhibited: high level of motives to use Tinder and
a high level of anxious attachment, a high level of urgency and sensation-seeking, and a
moderate level of self-esteem. This cluster had the highest level of problematic use and
the greater number of current matches whilst reporting an intermediate level of depressive
mood (Rochat et al., 2019). Tinder use in Cluster 3 subjects was prompted by a
combination of solid motivations (social, enhancement, and coping) and lacking self-
control, especially urgency, attributed to weak inhibitory control and poor decision-making
(Rochat et al., 2018).
They also have trouble overcoming the immediate gratification provided by the app, for
example, the excitement associated with potential sexual activity and reinforcement
resulting from frequent offline contacts, as indicated by their high sensation-seeking
levels. Accordingly, users with diminished self-control seem to be at higher risk for
developing problematic Tinder use, perhaps because of an interaction between poor self-
control and strong motives incentivising app use. People who had low self-esteem and a18
high level of avoidant attachment could be more inclined to use social networking sites
as these online environments seem safer to express themselves or meet potential
partners. Furthermore, the app can satisfy a need for social or sexual communication
without spatial (e.g., proximity) and social (e.g., possibility to unilaterally swipe left on a
potential match without consequences) constraints (Sumter et al., 2017). In addition, it
has been demonstrated that this subgroup has low conscientiousness, and it has been
observed that Tinder use has been associated with alleviating boredom (Orosz et al.,
2018).
There were limitations to this study, such as its cross-sectionalism. Research is required
to confirm the validity of the clusters longitudinally. In addition, the sample was self-
selected, hence limiting generalisability. It also relied on self-reports creating various
biases (e.g., social desirability and lack of insight). Also, the sample contained only
heterosexual-identifying subjects hence cannot be applied to non-heterosexual
participants (Rochat et al., 2019). Corroborating prior studies on internet gaming
depression (Billieux et al., 2015), the observations show that the dysfunctional use of
Tinder is heterogeneous and requires a wide variety of psychological variables that
interact. Further studies are needed to disentangle the role of these variables in problem
use growth, perpetuation, and recurrence and explain the very socially impactful appeal
this and similar dating apps have (Rochat et al., 2019).
Dispositional factors predicting use of online dating sites and behaviours related to online
dating
The use of online dating sites was investigated concerning many dispositional
considerations. The theory was that people who scored higher on extraversion,
neuroticism, self-esteem, and rejection sensitivity were more inclined to over-use a dating
site. Specifically, rejection sensitivity was a significant factor in predicting the use of online
dating sites, where personalities with higher rejection sensitivity were more likely to use
a dating site (Blackhart et al., 2014).
This is suggested to be due to the ease of avoiding potential rejection in an online dating
environment rather than in traditional dating environments. For example, online dating
platforms provide better opportunities for self-representations as users tend only to
present the pleasant side of themselves, therefore, reducing potential rejection.
Furthermore, it is easier to manage communication when communicating online, and
rejection online is less salient than face-to-face rejection (Blackhart et al., 2014).
The collective data offers some positive validation since traditionally, partnerships that
have been formed online have been frowned upon due to societal stigma. With recent
statistics showing cell phones becoming more popular and people’s lives becoming more
hectic than before, it is becoming a more appealing choice than meeting people19
organically. As with previous studies, it is proposed in this paper that the use of such
media can be healthy but has the potential to become harmful in the wrong hands, such
as those with dysfunctional attachment issues. In addition, if also potentially struggling
with specific mental health problems such as depression, a dating site can become a
coping mechanism and therefore has the propensity to cause further long-term harm by
deferring the real problem. Despite this, evidence indicates that a small group of
individuals, and, more importantly, the use of such websites, will typically form a long-
lasting and happy partnership.20
Conclusion
It is clear from the research that social networking is now important for humanity, from
running a business to finding a spouse to connecting with friends and family. The
neuropsychology research shows how well adapted social media suits our evolutionary
needs to keep up our reputation, observe and compare ourselves to other people and
feed our desire to be liked. This is reflected in the supply of such features on all the most
visited sites. The research shows that the combination of immediate gratification and relief
from loneliness and young age and genetic factors may result in potentially harmful
consequences such as dependence and addiction.
There are clear benefits of using social networking, primarily to offer worldwide social and
emotional support to people. With knowledge of the possibility of substantial damage to
more vulnerable individuals and awareness of the harmful functional properties of social
media features, several cautionary steps in an ethical direction could provide a well-
rounded, desirable platform.
To gain real social benefits and avoid the pitfalls of technology taking over genuine
relationships, a network that allows initial connectivity but quickly leads to face-to-face
contact would be an apparent alternative solution. In terms of amending what is already
there, this could be achieved by several approaches, including a focus on social and
emotional encouragement without the possibility of lurking other public accounts or
providing hollow validations with strategies such as liking.
Since all similar research has been conducted on a small scale, this article contains data
to justify a more extensive scale analysis into the usage and value of various social media
facets.21
